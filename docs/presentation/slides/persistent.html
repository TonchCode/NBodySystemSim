Persistent warps pull work from a software queue: per warp, per lane, or a static balanced split. Each one improved its balance metric.

But a warp queue scatters Morton neighbours over different SMs, and they stop sharing L1. A queue of 4-warp blocks recovers most of it (0.94–0.98×).

The hardware block scheduler is already a dynamic queue that keeps neighbours together. When the limit is L1, balance is not the goal.

This is the result I find most instructive. Persistent threads with work queues are a standard load-balancing technique. We built three versions on top of the tree: a warp-granular atomic queue, lane refill (idle lanes take the next body), and a static partition balanced on measured costs. Every one of them made its balance metric better, and every one ran slower: 0.63 to 0.97 of the plain launch. Why (exploratory diagnosis, not pre-registered): the hardware hands an SM a block of 4 Morton-adjacent warps that share L1 lines. The warp queue spreads them across SMs, and #5 is L1-bound, so that costs directly. A block-granular queue that keeps 4 neighbours together gets most of it back. Lane refill pays for warp-wide calls in every loop iteration; that explanation is not yet confirmed in SASS. Not a strawman: the lane-granular arm a reviewer asked for was built, verified bit for bit, and measured. Replicated at 0 % background load on 2026-10-18.
